The Engagement Trap

Recommender systems are trained to predict what you will click or rate highly. That number is a proxy: what we actually want is for people to find films they enjoy across the whole catalogue. This project asks what the proxy can and cannot see.

We built four recommenders on real MovieLens ratings, added a diversity-aware re-ranking step, and simulated thirty rounds of users clicking recommendations while the system retrained on those clicks. The answer: a click metric is blind to how narrow a recommender is. Two systems with the same click rate showed people very different amounts of the catalogue. Narrowness has to be measured separately, and a simple re-ranking step widens it at little cost.

Dataset
MovieLens ml-latest-small (GroupLens). 610 users; movies with at least 5 ratings, giving 3,650 movies and 90,274 ratings on a 0.5–5 star scale.
Algorithms compared
Linear Regression (ridge bias model) · Item-based KNN · SVD matrix factorisation · Content-based filtering on PCA-compressed genre and decade features. Popularity as a reference line.
Novelty
Diversity-aware re-ranking (Maximal Marginal Relevance, and cluster caps using K-Means vs. DBSCAN clusters), and a feedback-loop simulation whose hidden user model is a Logistic Regression.
Evaluation
Per-user 80/20 split, 3 seeds, hyperparameters tuned on a validation split carved from training data. RMSE, MAE, Precision/Recall/NDCG@10, catalogue coverage, exposure Gini, genre diversity, average popularity.
Code
github.com/paninishah/engagement-trap: four Kaggle notebooks, one shared library, and every result file shown on this page.

            How the models compare

            Test-set results, mean ± std over 3 seeds. The model that predicts star ratings best is not the one that makes the best top-10 lists.

            MMR gives a smooth dial: small amounts of variety cost very little accuracy. K-Means caps push harder against popular films. DBSCAN finds essentially one giant cluster in these embeddings, so capping by its clusters makes coverage worse. That is a real finding about density-based clustering, not a bug.

            How it works

            The pipeline

            1. Data. Ratings are filtered to films with at least 5 ratings, ids re-indexed, genres one-hot encoded and the release year parsed from the title.
            2. Four recommenders. Linear Regression learns a bias for every user and film. KNN predicts from the most similar films you rated. SVD factorises the mean-centred rating matrix into user and film vectors. The content model regresses each user's ratings on PCA components of film metadata.
            3. Re-ranking. MMR builds each list one film at a time, trading predicted relevance against similarity to films already chosen. Cluster caps limit how many films may come from one K-Means or DBSCAN cluster.
            4. Feedback loop. A Logistic Regression trained on genre affinity, mean ratings and popularity plays the hidden user. Its clicks, discounted by list position, become the next round's training data.

            What this page does live

            The SVD model was trained in the notebooks; this page only holds its 40-number vector for each film. When you pick films, your browser solves a small ridge regression to find the user vector that best explains liking them. This is called fold-in. Every film's score is the dot product of the two vectors. Re-ranking then runs on the top 100 candidates. Nothing is sent anywhere.

            Limits

            • Three seeds give means and spreads, not significance tests.
            • Offline metrics only reward films a user happened to rate, which favours popular films.
            • The validation rule picked a different "best" model than the test results; both are reported.
            • The simulation is a model of users, not real users: fixed tastes, an assumed position bias, and films never re-shown after a click.
            • Exposure stayed stuck rather than collapsing, and more variety did not help users reach more of their best matches.
